The integration of large language models (LLMs) with domain-speciﬁc computational tools provides a
pathway to streamline and enhance materials science workﬂows. This paper introduces MatSciAgent,
a multi-agent framework supporting tasks such as materials data retrieval, continuum simulation,
crystal structure generation, and molecular dynamics simulation. At its core is a master agent that
interprets user queries, identiﬁes the task type, and delegates to task-speciﬁc agent(s) equipped with
tools. Leveraging databases such as Materials Project and MatWeb, the framework retrieves and
summarizes materials data with grounded, factual responses, addressing limitations of vanilla LLMs.
When a target material is absent from databases, a generative agent can propose plausible crystal
structures. For simulations, specialized agents extract parameters to perform continuum and
molecular dynamics simulations using existing software or custom code. MatSciAgent demonstrates
stability, with parameter extraction achieving 100% success across ﬁve runs and materials extraction
consistent in 9 of 10 runs. Its modular design ensures seamless extensibility to evolve as new
capabilities are integrated.
Materials science and engineering are central to technological progress,
enabling the discovery, design, and optimization of materials tailored to
speciﬁc performance requirements. Emerging industries such as aerospace,
energy, and biotechnology increasingly demand materials with precisely
engineered mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties. Designing such
materials is a complex and iterative process that requires deep domain
expertise and advanced computational techniques. As a result, researchers
are increasingly leveraging computational tools to predict and optimize
material behavior, accelerating the development of materials with targeted
functionalities1,2.
This shift has driven the creation of large-scale materials databases
including the Materials Project3, MatWeb4, OQMD5, and ICSD6–which
serve as critical resources for data-driven materials discovery. Predictive
models built on these databases are increasingly used to guide materials
design decisions7. In particular, generative models–machine learning algorithms that learn patterns from data to generate new, plausible candidates
are being adopted to propose material compositions or structures, further
accelerating discovery8–10. However, despite these advancements, integrating various simulation tools and data sources into a uniﬁed framework

remains a signiﬁcant challenge. This lack of integration limits the seamless
use of computational resources and hinders the pace of innovation in
materials design11.
Recent advances in artiﬁcial intelligence–especially the emergence of
large language models (LLMs) have reshaped how researchers engage with
complex data and derive scientiﬁc insights. Built on transformer
architectures12, these models excel at understanding and generating natural
language through self-attention mechanisms, enabling scalable processing
of language-based information. LLMs, such as OpenAI’s GPT models13,14,
Meta’s LLaMA15, and Anthropic’s Claude16, have demonstrated exceptional
capabilities in interpreting user queries, synthesizing information from
diverse sources, and producing actionable insights. In the context of
materials science, LLMs support tasks such as information extraction from
large databases, literature mining, and hypothesis generation. Their ability
to process unstructured natural language queries allows researchers to
intuitively explore complex datasets, reducing manual effort and accelerating scientiﬁc workﬂows17–19. Additionally, transformer-based language
models have proven effective as encoders for property prediction tasks
across a range of scientiﬁc domains, including metal-organic frameworks20,

LLMs are now being integrated into agentic frameworks that extend
their utility from passive query interpretation to active scientiﬁc problemsolving29,30. These LLM agents leverage the model’s language understanding, pre-trained knowledge, and emerging planning abilities to
autonomously perform complex tasks. For instance, Boiko et al. introduced Coscientist, an LLM-driven agent that automates experimental
design and execution, substantially reducing manual effort31. Similarly,
ChemCrow combines LLMs with chemistry-speciﬁc tools to support
synthesis planning and materials discovery32. Such agentic systems are
being applied across diverse scientiﬁc domains, including not only materials science and chemistry but also ﬁelds like 3D printing and engineering
design33,34.
While LLMs demonstrate strong capabilities in natural language
processing and general reasoning, their application in specialized
scientiﬁc domains such as materials science presents several challenges.
One of the most signiﬁcant concerns is hallucination, where LLMs produce
information that appears plausible but is factually incorrect35–37. Such
outputs can mislead researchers, disrupt workﬂows, and lead to wasted
resources. This issue is particularly problematic when interpreting technical datasets, as LLMs are typically trained on general-purpose corpora
that lack sufﬁcient coverage of domain-speciﬁc materials science
knowledge38,39.
Furthermore, LLMs alone are inherently limited in delivering rigorous scientiﬁc predictions for complex physical phenomena, as such tasks
require high-precision, physics-based simulations and validated numerical
methods. To overcome this limitation, LLMs must be integrated with
domain-speciﬁc scientiﬁc software, enabling them to function as user

proxies that automate the setup and execution of simulation workﬂows.
Several hybrid frameworks have emerged to support this vision–LLaMP40,
HoneyComb41, and AtomAgents42 which couple LLMs with scientiﬁc tools
and databases. LLaMP and HoneyComb primarily function as retrieval
agents, leveraging data sources like the Materials Project, arXiv, and
Wikipedia. AtomAgents is a specialized intelligent system for alloy
development, integrating multimodal perception (e.g., microstructure
images, compositional data, phase diagrams) with domain-speciﬁc reasoning and materials design tools. Its core functionality lies in generating
and optimizing alloy compositions, predicting properties, and suggesting
processing pathways tailored towards performance requirements. As
research continues, there is a growing need for a more generalizable and
modular framework that can accommodate a broader range of materials
systems and simulation tasks, while remaining ﬂexible enough to incorporate newly developed computational tools.
We propose MatSciAgent, a multi-agent framework that integrates an
LLM with specialized computational tools to perform tasks based on natural
language user queries. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the system supports four task
types: materials retrieval, continuum simulation, crystal structure generation, and molecular dynamics simulation. Each task is handled by a dedicated task-speciﬁc agent, coordinated by a central master agent. The master
agent interprets the user’s input, determines the type of task, and delegates
the query to the appropriate task-speciﬁc agent. The assigned agent then
selects the relevant computational tools needed to complete the task. All
decisions, from task classiﬁcation to tool selection, are guided by the reasoning capabilities of the LLM. This framework demonstrates how LLMs
can enhance computational materials science by automating workﬂows,
improving accessibility to simulation tools, and accelerating research
processes.

Multi-agent system
The workﬂow consists of two key steps: First, when a user submits a query, a
master agent classiﬁes the task type and routes the query to the appropriate
task-speciﬁc agent. Then, the task-speciﬁc agent selects and deploys the
most suitable computational tools to solve the problem, delivering results
back to the user. Built on OpenAI’s GPT-3.5-turbo, it employs customized
prompts for domain-speciﬁc reasoning and tool integration. The modular
architecture supports diverse tasks, including data extraction, continuum
simulations, crystal structure generation, and molecular dynamics (MD)
simulations, ensuring easy expansion with new agents, tools, or workﬂows.
Each individual agent is powered by an LLM, equipped with taskspeciﬁc tools, and operates with shared memory supported by a scratchpad,
as shown in Fig. 2. A key strength is its agentic design, where each agent
operates independently and is equipped with specialized tools and prompts.
The scratchpad mechanism facilitates information exchange process within
the framework. When a user query enters the system, the MatSciAgent
selects the appropriate agent to handle the request. Initially empty, the
scratchpad begins recording the agent’s reasoning and actions. As the agent
identiﬁes a need for speciﬁc information, it invokes appropriate tools and
these invocations are added to intermediate steps. The tool executes and
returns results, which are then formatted and pushed back into the
scratchpad. Each time the agent is called, it receives this updated scratchpad
containing all previous tool calls and results, enabling continuous, coherent
reasoning. This process allows the agent to progressively build knowledge by
tracking previous ﬁndings, connecting different pieces of information, and
maintaining context throughout multi-step workﬂows. The agent continues
this cycle until it has gathered sufﬁcient information to form a ﬁnal response,
which is then extracted and presented to the user. The LLM serves as the
reasoning layer, dynamically interpreting queries and orchestrating tool use.
By combining LLM-driven reasoning with the precision of computation
tools, the system can handle complex queries, perform precise calculations,
and generate analyzable insights.

The task begins with the user query: "Provide synthesis details for perovskite
oxides used in solar cells.” (Full prompt and response shown in Supplementary Information Case 1.) A comparison between the materials
extraction agent and the vanilla GPT-3.5-turbo is summarized in Fig. 3. The
materials extraction agent ﬁrst identiﬁes key terms from the query namely
"perovskite” and "solar cells” using its reasoning capabilities. It then invokes
the materialsproject API to retrieve relevant data from the database.
This approach enables the agent to deliver more speciﬁc and detailed
information than the vanilla GPT-3.5-turbo. For example, it identiﬁes
speciﬁc compositions such as La0.8Sr0.2CoxFe1−xO3 and provides corresponding precursor details. It also outlines precise synthesis protocols,
including temperature conditions and duration. In contrast, GPT-3.5-turbo
returns only generic information and fails to offer detailed synthesis
procedures.

Continuum simulation ∣ Case 1: Al-Cu-Mg alloy solidiﬁcation with
diffusion-controlled growth
The user provides a query specifying the desired simulation conditions, as
illustrated in Fig. 6a. The agent interprets the input, extracts actionable
simulation parameters such as: width, attachment probability, and the
number of solid phases; and selects the appropriate tool–in this case, the CA
tool for simulating phase transformations. It then runs the CA simulation
using the derived parameters and generates a summary of the results,
including a visualization of the phase diagram. The evolution of the
microstructure is captured frame by frame, as shown in Fig. 6b, highlighting
randomly seeded nuclei distributed across the domain and forming dendritic growth patterns typical of diffusion-controlled solidiﬁcation.
Continuum simulation ∣ Case 2: Ti-Al intermetallic alloy solidiﬁcation with interface-controlled growth
In this case study, the continuum simulation agent is applied to model
interface-controlled growth in an alloy system. The agent interprets the user’s
natural language query, extracts the relevant simulation parameters, and
executes a CA simulation to generate frame-by-frame images of microstructural evolution during solidiﬁcation, as shown in Fig. 7. The simulation
results reveal smooth, isotropic growth behavior, characterized by uniformly
expanding grains without the formation of dendritic arms–consistent with
the high attachment probability conditions. Controlled nucleation, set at 50
nuclei per unit area, leads to evenly distributed grains across the domain,
resulting in a homogeneous microstructure. The high attachment probability
also enhances solid-liquid interface kinetics, promoting the formation of
uniformly sized grains with minimal solute segregation.

Continuum simulation ∣ Case 3: Polycrystalline grain growth
using Monte Carlo annealing
This task involves performing an MCA simulation to model polycrystalline grain growth as shown in Fig. 8. Unlike the previous two
case studies, the agent applies the MCA simulation tool based on the
description provided in the user query. The simulation, conducted on
a 50 × 50 lattice grid, demonstrates classic grain coarsening
behavior–larger grains grow at the expense of smaller ones. The
lattice initially contains randomly oriented grains, but over the
course of 100 Monte Carlo steps, the system evolves to minimize
interfacial energy. Grain boundaries shift, especially at interfaces
where different grains meet, driving the reduction of total grain
boundary energy. This results in a coarser microstructure, characterized by fewer, larger grains–consistent with the typical grain
growth behavior observed during the annealing of polycrystalline
materials.

Fig. 9(b). Based on the CIF ﬁle, the agent constructs a natural language response that includes precise lattice parameters (a = b = c =
3.5555) and atomic positions (Al at (0, 0, 0) and Ni at (0, 0.5, 0.5)),
consistent with the speciﬁed symmetry and stoichiometry. In addition to atomic coordinates, the CIF ﬁle also contains elemental
properties such as electronegativity and ionic radii.

Molecular dynamics ∣ Case 1: MD simulation of aluminum at
constant energy
In this case, the molecular dynamics agent performs an MD simulation of
aluminum (Al). It interprets the natural language query to extract simulation conditions, identifying a constant energy (NVE) ensemble with a target
temperature of 660°C, which it correctly converts to 933.15 K, as illustrated

The system is initialized using a face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal

modeled using the Effective Medium Theory (EMT) potential, as speciﬁed
in the query. The simulation runs for 100,000 steps (Fig. 11b). The results
show that the system’s total energy remains constant throughout the
simulation, consistent with the expected behavior of an NVE ensemble. The
temperature ﬂuctuates around the target value, with kinetic and potential

crystal structures. This agent invoked the CrystaLLM tool with the speciﬁed parameters (composition = ‘Ni1Al1’, space group = ‘Pm-3m’) and
successfully produced the corresponding CIF ﬁle.
In the second stage, a follow-up prompt requested an MD simulation of
the generated AlNi structure under NVT conditions, using the EMT

100,000 steps. The corresponding MD trajectory can be found in Supplementary Data 2. The system again selected the appropriate agent MolecularDynamicsAgent which used the MDSimulationNVTTool to
carry out the simulation, automatically extracting relevant parameters from
the previously generated CIF ﬁle.

Reliability analysis ∣ Materials extraction
To evaluate the consistency of the LLM agent, we performed 10 independent
runs of the same prompt. All runs selected: MaterialExtractionAgent and
tools: get_material_ids and MaterialsProjectBonds. Among the 10 runs,
runs 0–4 and 6–9 yielded identical sets of 47 material IDs. Run 5 failed to
extract any material IDs, suggesting an isolated error. The consistency in the
other nine runs demonstrates the stability and reproducibility of the
material extraction component of the agent.

Conclusion
The integration of LLMs with simulation tools demonstrates their
growing utility in computational materials science. This paper showcases
their effectiveness in structure retrieval and generation, as well as in
running continuum and MD simulations. The case studies highlight the
ﬂexibility of LLMs in automating workﬂows that incorporate both custom
code and established software. The agent consistently extracts relevant
Communications Materials | (2026)7:131

parameters and search keywords from natural language input with high
accuracy.
The reliability analysis demonstrates that the framework maintains
consistent agent and tool selection, parameter extraction, and simulation
execution across repeated runs. With near-perfect reproducibility in most
tasks and only minor, non-impacting variations in formatting or isolated
extraction failures, the system shows the stability required for materials
research.
A key strength of the framework is its modular design, which allows it
to expand as new tools are added. Broadening data sources beyond the
Materials Project and MatWeb would further enhance its applicability.
Simulation capabilities could be improved through advanced workﬂows,
multiscale modeling, and ML-guided optimization. Moreover, enabling the
agent to dynamically write or adapt code would increase its ﬂexibility,
making it better suited for unseen or specialized tasks.

Materials extraction agent
LLMs, while powerful, are typically trained on general-purpose natural
language datasets and lack access to the specialized, structured data required
for materials science applications46,47. Because they rely on static, pre-trained
knowledge, they cannot dynamically query authoritative databases, often
resulting in outdated or incomplete information. Furthermore, their
probabilistic nature can lead to overgeneralization or misinterpretation of
complex scientiﬁc queries48.
To overcome these limitations, we introduce a materials extraction
agent that integrates the Materials Project API and custom web-scraping for
MatWeb, enabling real-time access to validated, domain-speciﬁc data.
Upon receiving a user query, the agent retrieves relevant material
properties–such as thermal, dielectric, and surface characteristics–by
invoking the appropriate database call, either via API or web scraping. It
then synthesizes a natural language response based on the retrieved data.
This method ensures that responses are grounded in current, authoritative
sources, allowing for more accurate contextualization of materials, veriﬁcation of properties under speciﬁc conditions, and delivery of reliable,
data-driven insights critical for real-world applications. Importantly, by
relying on scientiﬁcally validated sources, the system also enhances the
reproducibility of output prompt generation.
The agent retrieves data from structured databases, including the
Materials Project3 and MatWeb4. The Materials Project offers access to over
150,000 materials with properties such as elasticity, phonon spectra, and
thermodynamic stability–essential for predictive modeling. MatWeb complements this with experimentally validated material data, enabling direct
comparisons between computational predictions and empirical behavior.
Further implementation details are provided in the Methods section.

Monte Carlo annealing
Monte Carlo Annealing is used to simulate grain growth or spin ordering in
a two-dimensional Potts model at very low temperatures (T ≈ 0), where the
system dynamics are governed primarily by energy minimization50. This
corresponds to zero-temperature Metropolis dynamics, in which spin ﬂips
are only accepted if they lower or maintain the system’s energy. Each site on
the 2D lattice can exist in one of Q possible states, and the system evolves by
locally minimizing interfacial energy between different states.
The model is characterized by several key parameters. The variable Q
deﬁnes the number of possible states or “ﬂavors” per lattice site, which can
represent, for example, distinct grain orientations in a polycrystalline
material. The parameter L sets the size of the square lattice, resulting in a
total of L × L sites. The simulation proceeds for a given number of Monte
Carlo steps, with each step consisting of L2 spin-ﬂip attempts. The biasing
strategy determines how both the site and the trial state are selected during
simulation. Strategies include selecting a random new state from all Q
options, choosing from neighboring states at any site, or restricting to
neighboring states only at interfacial sites (i.e., those bordering unlike
neighbors).
This simulation produces several outputs that help analyze the
system’s behavior. The energy history tracks how the total energy of
the lattice evolves over time, typically decreasing as the system anneals.
The acceptance history records the number of accepted spin ﬂips per
Monte Carlo step, providing insight into how active or “frozen” the
system becomes during the simulation. The conﬁguration history
stores snapshots of the lattice at each step, allowing visualization of
the evolving microstructure and phase ordering. If plotting is enabled,
the simulation also generates visualizations of the energy curve, acceptance
rate, and both initial and ﬁnal conﬁgurations. Additionally, it can save
all conﬁguration snapshots as images in a results directory for further
analysis.

Cellular automata
In this work, the Cellular Automata model is used to simulate phase
transformations such as solidiﬁcation or recrystallization within a twodimensional, grid-based system. Each cell in the grid represents a point in
space and can exist either in the liquid phase, denoted by 0, or in one of
several solid phases, represented by positive integers. The transformation
process is driven by local interactions between neighboring cells and governed by an attachment probability, allowing the model to effectively capture the evolution of microstructures during phase changes.

interior cells to avoid boundary effects. The attach_prob parameter

Continuum simulation agent
The continuum simulation agent executes advanced materials science
simulations, integrating two key modeling techniques: Cellular Automata
(CA)51,52 for phase transformations and Monte Carlo Annealing (MCA)50,53
for microstructural evolution during annealing. Users provide simulation
conditions in natural language, which the agent translates into actionable
parameters before executing the simulation.
The CA tool models phase transformations such as solidiﬁcation and
recrystallization by iteratively updating a 2D grid based on local neighborhood conditions. It accounts for attachment probabilities and tracks
transformed regions over time, offering insights into grain growth and
nucleation in both single-phase and multiphase systems.
MCA tool, using the Potts model54, simulates the evolution of the grain
structure under annealing conditions. Operating near zero temperature, it
employs three biasing strategies: unbiased selection, neighboring ﬂavor

CrystaLLM
CrystaLLM is a domain-speciﬁc LLM designed to generate crystallographic
structures in CIF format from basic compositional input. Built on the GPT-2
architecture and adapted using the nanoGPT implementation55,56, CrystaLLM has been purposefully trained for applications in inorganic materials
science. Rather than relying on general-purpose vocabularies, it uses a
custom byte-level tokenizer with a vocabulary of 371 tokens tailored to the
syntactic patterns of CIF ﬁles.
The training dataset for CrystaLLM was assembled from multiple
high-quality materials science databases, including the Materials Project3,
the Open Quantum Materials Database5, and the NOMAD57. These sources
provide a diverse range of inorganic crystal structures, enabling the model to
learn detailed patterns related to atomic arrangements, symmetry operations, and lattice parameters across a wide variety of crystal systems.
We employ the small variant of CrystaLLM, with temperature set to
0.8; top-k sampling with k = 10; random seed ﬁxed at 1337 for reproducibility; executed with bﬂoat16 in our study. This version consists of 8
transformer layers, each with 8 attention heads and a context window of
1,024 tokens. The model has an embedding dimension of 512 and was
trained from scratch for 5,000 iterations.
Crystal structure generation agent
Atomic structure is fundamental to understanding inorganic crystal materials and performing computational simulations. When structural data is
available in existing datasets, it can be directly retrieved. However, if such
data is missing, alternative methods are needed to generate the desired
atomic structures.
The crystal generator agent addresses this by generating crystal structures
based on elemental compositions or alloy systems using CrystaLLM,
a transformer-based language model speciﬁcally designed to produce
CIFs (Crystallographic Information Files) of inorganic crystals8. Upon
receiving a user query, the agent extracts relevant information and runs
CrystaLLM to generate plausible inorganic crystal structures. It can produce
multiple candidate structures for a given composition, enabling material
exploration.
Unlike general-purpose LLMs, CrystaLLM is tailored to recognize
complex crystallographic patterns, enabling the generation of physically
realistic structures. As part of a multi-agent system, the crystal generator
agent streamlines materials workﬂows by providing immediate input for
downstream tasks such as thermodynamic stability analysis, property prediction, and MD simulations. While CrystaLLM is presented here as an
example generative model for crystal structure generation based on composition and space group information note that the framework is modular
and can accommodate additional generative models, including those capable of property-constrained generation, allowing future extensions toward
inverse materials design as well.
Molecular dynamics simulation
MD simulations were conducted using the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) to investigate the structural evolution of atomic systems under
various thermodynamic conditions. The agentic framework enables the
execution of MD simulations in NVE (constant energy), NVT (constant
Number, Volume, Temperature), and NPT (constant Number, Pressure,
Temperature) ensembles. Thermodynamic control was achieved using the
Communications Materials | (2026)7:131

Berendsen thermostat for temperature regulation and the Berendsen
barostat for pressure regulation in NPT simulations.
Key simulation parameters include the time step for numerical integration, target temperature, and the total number of MD steps. For ensemble
control, the thermostat and barostat time constants were speciﬁed, along
with external pressure and barostat coupling strength for NPT conditions.
Additionally, a logging interval was deﬁned to record system properties and
trajectories at regular time steps. Simulation outputs consist of atomic trajectories saved in .traj format, log ﬁles detailing the evolution of energy,
temperature, and stress, and corresponding visualizations of energy and
temperature proﬁles over time.
Molecular dynamics agent
Atomic-scale simulations are essential for understanding fundamental
material behaviors, such as thermal conductivity, mechanical strength, and
phase transitions58,59. The MD simulation agent is designed to automate and
streamline atomic-scale simulations. It interprets user input queries, sets up
the appropriate simulation parameters, and executes the MD simulation
accordingly. Speciﬁcally, the agent initializes atomic structures, deﬁnes
interatomic potentials, and runs simulations using tools primarily based on
the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) package60.
It supports the construction of atomic structures with various crystal
symmetries and accommodates a range of interatomic potentials, including
the Embedded Atom Method (EAM)61, Lennard-Jones (LJ)62, and Effective
Medium Theory (EMT)63. These enable simulations of metals, alloys, and
simple molecular systems. The agent can also perform simulations under
different thermodynamic ensembles–NVE (constant energy), NVT (constant temperature), and NPT (constant pressure)–allowing users to explore
material behavior under controlled conditions. Further details are provided
in the Methods section. By translating natural language input into
simulation-ready parameters, the agent simpliﬁes the MD simulation
workﬂow.
Integrating the MD simulation tools with an LLM agent signiﬁcantly
enhances its capabilities. Users can interactively conﬁgure simulations, analyze results, and visualize atomic trajectories using natural language queries.
Unlike traditional standalone MD tools, this agent automates the entire
workﬂow–from structure initialization to result interpretation–providing
actionable outputs such as energy plots and trajectory data with minimal
manual effort.
